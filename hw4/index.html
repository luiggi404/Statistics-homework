HW4 — The penetration score as a random walk

n systems, m attackers, i.i.d. increments. Simulated trajectories of the total, average and normalised score; the Law of Large Numbers, the variance under three scalings, and the Central Limit Theorem — all computed live in your browser.

What this page is about. Adding up many small independent random scores produces three very different objects depending on how you scale the sum. Divided by nothing, the total Sn wanders off and spreads out. Divided by n, it freezes onto a constant (Law of Large Numbers). Divided by √n — and only by √n — it settles into a stable bell-shaped law (Central Limit Theorem). The homework is to see all three happen on simulated attackers and to read them as statements about attacks on a population of systems.

0. The model

Definition. A random walk is the sequence of partial sums Sk = X1 + … + Xk of independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) increments Xi. One realisation k ↦ Sk is a sample trajectory.

How the n systems and m attackers enter. Each of the m attackers works through the n systems one at a time. At system i the attacker earns a score Xi; the attacker's running score after k systems is Sk. So every attacker is one sample trajectory of length n, and the m attackers are m independent copies of the process. The distribution of a quantity "at size n" is read across the m attackers at step n.

  • Bernoulli model, Xi ∈ {0, 1}, P(Xi = 1) = p: Xi = 1 means system i was penetrated. Sn is the number of breached systems, Sn/n the fraction breached. Mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1 − p).
  • Rademacher model, Xi = ±1 with probability ½ each: a step forward (foothold gained) or backward (detected, access lost). Sn is the attacker's net progress. Mean μ = 0, variance σ² = 1: the symmetric, zero-mean case.

Both models are simulated with the same n and m. Every attacker is observed at the checkpoints n = 10, 20, 50, 100, … up to the maximum, so "increasing n" means watching the same attackers progress through more systems. The generator is seeded (xoshiro128**), so a seed reproduces a run exactly.

1. What theory predicts

Definitions. A sequence Yn converges in probability to a constant c if, for every ε > 0, P(|Yn − c| > ε) → 0. It converges in distribution to a law with CDF F if P(Yn ≤ x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of F. The first says the values themselves pile up at c; the second only says the histogram takes a limiting shape.

By linearity of expectation and independence (variances of independent terms add):

Why √n is the only "stable" scaling. For any power α, Var(Sn/nα) = σ² n1−2α. If α < ½ the spread explodes, if α > ½ it collapses to zero (α = 1 is the LLN), and only α = ½ keeps it finite and non-zero. That is why the CLT lives at √n.

The two theorems used below.

  • Weak LLN (via Chebyshev's inequality): P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(Sn/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0.
  • CLT: (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution. For Rademacher, nμ = 0 and σ = 1, so this is Sn/√n. For Bernoulli, Sn/√n must first be centred by √n p and scaled by σ.
  • Berry–Esseen (how fast): supx |P(Zn ≤ x) − Φ(x)| ≤ C·ρ/(σ³√n) with ρ = E|X − μ|³ and C ≤ 0.4748. For Rademacher ρ = 1, so the error is at most 0.4748/√n.

4. Law of Large Numbers: Sn/n → μ in probability

For each n we estimate P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) as the fraction of the m attackers whose average score misses μ by more than ε, and compare it with Chebyshev's bound σ²/(nε²) and with the normal approximation 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)).

  • The probability goes to 0 for both models, as the weak LLN requires. The tolerance ε is arbitrary: a smaller ε only delays the fall, by a factor 1/ε² in n.
  • Chebyshev is valid but loose. It only uses the variance, so it decays like 1/n, while the true probability decays exponentially (Hoeffding: ≤ 2e−2nε² for Bernoulli). The normal approximation tracks the simulation closely once n is moderate.
  • Rademacher concentrates more slowly in absolute terms because σ² = 1 > p(1 − p): the bigger the per-step noise, the more systems are needed for the same precision on the average.
  • In probability vs along a path. The trajectory chart of Sk/k (section 2) shows something stronger: every individual path settles onto μ (strong LLN, almost-sure convergence). The weak LLN only talks about the fraction of paths outside the ε-band at a fixed n.

5. The variance under the three normalisations

Sample variance across the m attackers at each n (points) against the theoretical nσ², σ²/n and σ² (lines), on log–log axes where a power law nβ is a straight line of slope β.

  • Slope +1 for Sn: the total gets more uncertain as n grows; its standard deviation grows like √n (while for Bernoulli its mean grows like n, so the relative spread still shrinks).
  • Slope −1 for Sn/n: the variance of the average falls like 1/n — this is precisely the mechanism behind the LLN.
  • Slope 0 for Sn/√n: the variance is σ² at every n. This is the scaling where neither spreading nor concentration wins.
  • The scatter of the points around the lines is the estimation error of the sample variance, ≈ Var·√(2/m). It does not depend on n: m alone controls it.

6. Central Limit Theorem: Sn/√n → N(0, 1)

The distance used is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance Dn = supx|Fn(x) − Φ(x)|, the largest vertical gap between a CDF and the standard normal CDF Φ. Convergence in distribution to N(0, 1) means Dn → 0 (Φ is continuous everywhere).

n =
  • Rademacher: Sn/√n itself converges. The exact distance (computed from the binomial law, no simulation noise) falls like 1/√n and stays under the Berry–Esseen bound 0.4748/√n. Its leading term is half the central jump of the CDF, about 0.4/√n.
  • It converges although every Sn/√n is discrete. Rademacher Sn only takes values of the same parity as n, so Sn/√n lives on a grid of spacing 2/√n. The grid gets finer, the jumps get smaller, and the staircase CDF approaches Φ — convergence in distribution never needed a density.
  • Bernoulli without centring does not converge. Sn/√n has the right variance p(1 − p) but its mean √n p runs off to infinity, so its distance from N(0, 1) tends to 1. Centre and scale it, (Sn − np)/√(np(1−p)), and it converges just like Rademacher (more slowly when p is far from ½: the skewness enters ρ).
  • The simulation cannot see below its own noise. With m attackers, an empirical CDF wobbles around the true one by about 0.87/√m (dashed grey line). Once the true distance drops below that floor, the measured Dn stops falling: resolving the CLT at size n needs m of order 5n or more.

7. What the number of attackers m changes

Because attackers are independent copies, m does not change the law of any single attacker's score. It changes three other things.

7.1 The precision of everything we measure

All of these shrink like 1/√m: four times as many attackers halve every error bar. A "suitable" m is therefore set by the smallest effect one wants to see — not by n.

7.2 The strongest attacker

A defender rarely cares about the average attacker, but about the best one. If Zj = (Sn(j) − nμ)/(σ√n) are approximately N(0, 1) by the CLT, the best of m attackers sits at about E[max of m normals] ≈ √(2 ln m) standard deviations above the mean.

7.3 All attackers together

The pooled score of the whole population, T = Sn(1) + … + Sn(m), is itself a sum of m·n i.i.d. increments. Every law above applies to it with n replaced by m·n: its average T/(mn) concentrates at μ with standard deviation σ/√(mn).

8. Interpretation: system size and number of attackers

  • Larger infrastructures make the breach rate predictable, not the breach count. In the Bernoulli model the number of breached systems grows like np, and its uncertainty grows too, like √(np(1−p)). But the fraction breached concentrates on p with error ~1/√n. A defender of a large estate can forecast the fraction of systems that will fall with good precision; the absolute number keeps a √n margin of error.
  • Attackers become indistinguishable in rate, but not in count. As n grows, the spread of Sn/n across attackers vanishes — every attacker's success rate tends to the same p — while the gap in Sn between the best and worst attacker widens like √n. On a big network, "how many more systems did the best attacker get" keeps growing even though "how much better is their rate" goes to zero.
  • The zero-mean case still produces large individual scores. With Rademacher increments there is no systematic progress (E[Sn] = 0, Sn/n → 0), yet a typical attacker ends about √(2/π)·√n ≈ 0.8√n steps away from the start, in one direction or the other, and the trajectories show long excursions. Zero average does not mean that nobody gets far; it means that gains and setbacks balance on average over attackers.
  • More attackers raise the worst case, slowly. m does not change any single attacker's distribution, but the strongest of m attackers reaches nμ + σ√n·√(2 ln m) approximately. Doubling the system size n multiplies the extra margin by √2; going from 10 to 1000 attackers only multiplies it by about 2 (the expected maximum grows from 1.54σ to 3.24σ). System size dominates; attacker count enters logarithmically.
  • Aggregated over the population, the noise disappears. The pooled breach rate of all m attackers on all n systems has error σ/√(mn): with many attackers and many systems, the aggregate penetration score is essentially deterministic, which is why aggregate statistics (incident counts across a sector) are far more stable than any single organisation's.
  • When this breaks. All of the above rests on independence and identical distribution. If systems share a vulnerability, or attackers coordinate, increments become positively correlated: Var(Sn) grows faster than n, Sn/n can stay random (e.g. if each attacker has their own skill pj, Sn/n → pj, a random limit), and the normal approximation underestimates extreme scores. The i.i.d. model is the baseline against which such dependence is detected.
