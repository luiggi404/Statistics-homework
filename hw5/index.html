HW5 — Welford's recurrences and the sampling mean and variance

Exercise 1 derives a one-pass, numerically stable update for the mean, the sum of squares and the variance. Exercise 2 gives every penetration attempt a score from 0 to 10, runs that update along each hacker's trajectory, and checks the estimates against the true μ and σ² for growing n.

What this page is about. A statistic computed from data (the sample mean, the sample variance) is itself a random variable: run the experiment again and you get a different value. Its distribution over repetitions is the sampling distribution. Here every hacker is one repetition. Averaging a statistic over many hackers tells us whether it is right on average (bias); its spread across hackers tells us how far a single value can be trusted (variance). Both are exactly what inferential statistics needs to go from one sample to a statement about the population.

Exercise 1 — Welford's recurrence formulas

1.1 Starting point: Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Definition. Given observations x1, x2, …, the running mean after k of them is Mk = (x1 + … + xk)/k. A recurrence computes Mk from Mk−1 and the new value xk alone, so the data never has to be stored or re-read.

Write the sum in two ways: k·Mk = (k−1)·Mk−1 + xk. Dividing by k and rearranging:

Mk = ((k−1)·Mk−1 + xk) / k = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k, M0 = 0

The update is "old mean + (surprise of the new point) / k". The two facts it rests on, and that we will reuse, are

(a) k·Mk = (k−1)·Mk−1 + xk (b) k·(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1

1.2 The same idea for the sum of squares

Definition. The sum of squares (of deviations from the mean) of the first k observations is SSk = Σi=1..k (xi − Mk)². It measures the total spread around the current mean; the variance is SS divided by k (population form) or by k−1 (sample form).

The difficulty compared with the mean: when xk arrives the mean moves, so every old deviation xi − Mk−1 changes. We need the net effect of that shift in closed form. Expanding the square gives the algebraic identity SSk = Σ xi² − k·Mk², used here only to find the increment:

  1. Subtract the identity at k−1 from the identity at k; the old xi² cancel:
    SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k−1)·Mk−1²
  2. By (a), (k−1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk, so (k−1)·Mk−1² = Mk−1·(k·Mk − xk):
    SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − xk·Mk−1 − k·Mk·(Mk − Mk−1)
  3. By (b), k·(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1, so the last term is Mk·(xk − Mk−1):
    SSk − SSk−1 = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − Mk·(xk − Mk−1)

Collecting the common factor gives Welford's recurrence:

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk), SS0 = 0

It has the same shape as Knuth's: old value plus a correction that uses only the new point and the two means. The correction is the product of the deviation of xk from the old mean and from the new mean — both are known at step k, and both have the same sign (Mk lies between Mk−1 and xk), so SS can only grow, as a sum of squares must.

Using xk − Mk = (xk − Mk−1) − (Mk − Mk−1) = (xk − Mk−1)·(k−1)/k, an equivalent form with a single deviation δ = xk − Mk−1 is

SSk = SSk−1 + δ²·(k−1)/k

1.3 The recurrence for the variance

Definition. The sample variance is sk² = SSk/(k−1) (k ≥ 2); the population (or biased) variance is σ̂k² = SSk/k. Exercise 2 shows why the k−1 divisor is the right one for estimation.

Dividing the second form of Welford's recurrence by k−1, and writing SSk−1 = (k−2)·sk−1²:

sk² = ((k−2)/(k−1))·sk−1² + (xk − Mk−1)² / k, k ≥ 2 (s1² is undefined, its coefficient is 0)

and dividing by k instead, for the population variance, in the "old + correction / k" form of Knuth's mean:

σ̂k² = σ̂k−1² + ((xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk) − σ̂k−1²) / k, σ̂0² = 0

In practice one keeps SS as the state and divides only when the variance is needed: it avoids the special case at k = 1 and costs nothing.

1.4 The code

The core is three lines: compute the deviation from the old mean, update the mean (Knuth), update SS with the product of the old and new deviations (Welford).

JavaScript — the exact lines used in the simulation below

const d = x - mean;       // x_k − M_{k−1}
mean += d / k;            // M_k   (Knuth)
ss   += d * (x - mean);   // SS_k  (Welford)

Python

d = x - mean
mean += d / k
ss += d * (x - mean)

Wrapped into a streaming accumulator:

def welford(stream):
    k, mean, ss = 0, 0.0, 0.0
    for x in stream:
        k += 1
        d = x - mean
        mean += d / k
        ss += d * (x - mean)
    return mean, ss / (k - 1), ss / k      # mean, sample variance, population variance

1.5 Trying it, and why not the textbook formula

Type any list of numbers. The table runs the three lines one observation at a time.

Why a recurrence instead of Σx² − (Σx)²/n? The "one-pass textbook" formula SS = Σxi² − (Σxi)²/n is exact algebra but disastrous arithmetic: when the mean is large compared with the spread, it subtracts two huge, nearly equal numbers and loses all significant digits (catastrophic cancellation). The two-pass formula Σ(xi − x̄)² is accurate but has to read the data twice. Welford reads it once and only ever handles deviations from the current mean, which are of the size of the spread, not of the data. Below, the data above is shifted by a growing constant c: the true variance does not change.

All computations in IEEE-754 double precision (53-bit mantissa, about 16 significant digits). Once Σx² exceeds 2⁵³ ≈ 9·10¹⁵, the spacing between representable doubles exceeds 1, and the textbook formula can even return a negative variance. At c = 10¹² Welford also drifts slightly: there the input numbers themselves can only be stored to about 10⁻⁴, so no algorithm can recover more digits; the error comes from the data, not from the recurrence.

Exercise 2 — Sampling mean and variance of the penetration scores

2.0 The model

Definition. Each penetration attempt receives a score X ∈ {0, 1, …, 10} drawn from a fixed probability mass function p(k) = P(X = k). Its theoretical mean is μ = Σ k·p(k) and its variance σ² = Σ (k − μ)²·p(k). The scores of all attempts are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

How hackers and systems enter. As in HW4, each of the m hackers attempts the n systems in order and gets one score per system: a trajectory X1, …, Xn. Hacker j runs Welford's recurrence along their own trajectory and ends with a sampling mean x̄j = Mn and a sampling variance sj² = SSn/(n−1). The m hackers are m independent repetitions of the same experiment, so the m values x̄1, …, x̄m are a sample from the sampling distribution of x̄ (and likewise for s²).

The default choice is Binomial(10, p): an attempt is 10 independent stages, each passed with probability p, and the score is the number of stages passed. It extends HW4 directly (p = 0.3 there too), with μ = 10p and σ² = 10p(1 − p). Three other distributions are available to show which conclusions depend on the shape and which do not.

Every hacker is observed at n = 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, … up to the maximum: "repeating the experiment for different n" means reading the Welford state of the same hackers after more systems. Results at different n are therefore correlated with each other, while hackers are independent of each other. The generator is seeded (xoshiro128**, shared with HW4).

2.1 What theory predicts

Definitions. An estimator θ̂ of a parameter θ is unbiased if E[θ̂] = θ: averaged over infinitely many repetitions it hits the target. Its standard error is its standard deviation √Var(θ̂). It is consistent if θ̂ → θ in probability as the sample size grows.

Sampling mean. By linearity, E[x̄] = μ. By independence, Var(x̄) = σ²/n. So x̄ is unbiased and its standard error is σ/√n.

Sampling variance. Using SS = Σxi² − n·x̄², E[X²] = σ² + μ² and E[x̄²] = σ²/n + μ²:

E[SS] = n(σ² + μ²) − n(σ²/n + μ²) = (n − 1)·σ² ⇒ E[s²] = E[SS/(n−1)] = σ² E[σ̂²] = E[SS/n] = σ²·(n−1)/n

One degree of freedom is "used up" by measuring deviations from x̄ instead of from the unknown μ: the data are always closer to their own mean than to μ, so SS underestimates the spread by exactly one σ². Dividing by n − 1 corrects this; dividing by n leaves a bias of −σ²/n.

The spread of s² depends on the fourth central moment μ4 = E[(X − μ)⁴], i.e. on the tails of the score distribution:

Var(s²) = ( μ4 − σ⁴·(n−3)/(n−1) ) / n ≈ σ⁴·(κ − 1)/n κ = μ4/σ⁴ (kurtosis) Cov(x̄, s²) = μ3/n μ3 = E[(X − μ)³] (zero for symmetric scores)

Averages over the m hackers. The average of the m sampling means, (1/m)·Σ x̄j, has mean μ and standard error σ/√(nm); the average of the m sampling variances has mean σ² and standard error √(Var(s²)/m). These standard errors are the yardstick for "close": a difference of a couple of standard errors is noise, a difference of many is bias.

2.6 Using the estimates for inference: confidence intervals

The practical reason to care about x̄ and s²: from one hacker's n scores, without knowing μ or σ, one can build the 95 % interval x̄ ± tn−1·s/√n. Each hacker builds their own; the fraction of hackers whose interval contains the true μ is the empirical coverage, which should be 95 %. The same with the normal quantile 1.96 instead of Student's t shows what ignoring the uncertainty in s costs.

3. Conclusions

What the comparisons show

  • The average of the sampling means matches μ at every n, from n = 2 upward, within its standard error. x̄ is unbiased regardless of n and regardless of the shape of the score distribution: unbiasedness needs only linearity of expectation.
  • The average of the sampling variances (n − 1 divisor) matches σ² at every n, again within its standard error and for every distribution. Dividing SS by n instead gives averages that sit visibly below σ², by the predicted factor (n − 1)/n: halved at n = 2, 10 % low at n = 10, negligible only for large n. This is the experimental proof that n − 1 is the correct divisor.
  • Unbiased does not mean accurate for one hacker. A single hacker's x̄ and s² can be far from μ and σ² (sections 2.2 and 2.5), especially for small n. Unbiasedness is a statement about the average over repetitions — which is exactly what the m hackers provide.
  • The estimates are not independent of each other in general. For skewed scores, hackers with a high x̄ also tend to have a high s² (Cov = μ3/n > 0). Independence of x̄ and s² is a special property of normal data.

Why this is useful in inferential statistics

  • We never see μ and σ²; we only see one sample. Inference replaces them by x̄ and s². Knowing that the replacements are unbiased and consistent is what justifies the substitution: on average they are right, and with more data they converge.
  • The sampling distribution gives the error bars. Var(x̄) = σ²/n, estimated by s²/n, gives the standard error s/√n of a single estimate. With the CLT (the histogram of x̄ in 2.5 is close to normal already at moderate n even for a skewed or bimodal score), this produces confidence intervals and tests — and section 2.6 shows they deliver the promised 95 % coverage once n is not tiny.
  • Unbiased s² is the ingredient of Student's t. The t-interval and the t-test use s² with n − 1 degrees of freedom precisely because of the result above; using the biased σ̂² or the normal quantile undercovers at small n (2.6).
  • Welford makes all of this possible in a stream. A security monitor that scores every attempt in real time can maintain x̄ and s² (and so an interval for the mean score) with O(1) memory per source and without the numerical breakdown of Σx² − n·x̄².

Effect of the number of systems n

  • The variability of the sampling means falls like 1/n (standard deviation like 1/√n), exactly σ²/n: slope −1 in 2.4. Four times as many systems halve the uncertainty on a hacker's mean score. This is the Law of Large Numbers of HW4 seen through the variance.
  • The variability of the sampling variances also falls like 1/n, but with a constant set by the tails: Var(s²) ≈ σ⁴(κ − 1)/n. Heavy-tailed scores (the skewed distribution, κ ≈ 6) need several times more systems than light-tailed ones (uniform κ ≈ 1.8, bimodal κ ≈ 1.2) for the same relative precision on σ². Estimating a spread is always harder than estimating a centre.
  • Both sampling distributions become narrower and more normal as n grows (2.5): s² is strongly right-skewed at small n and close to a bell at large n.
  • n and m do different jobs. n controls how precise each hacker's estimate is; m controls how precisely we can measure the average and the spread of those estimates across hackers (the standard errors of the averages shrink like 1/√m). Bias is visible when it is larger than about 2·SE, which is why the (n−1)/n bias of SS/n is detectable at n = 2…50 but drowns in noise at n = 1 000.
