HW7 — Statistical independence and Pearson's χ² test

Given a bivariate dataset of categorical observations (xk, fk), the page builds the contingency table by counting the records, derives joint, marginal and conditional frequencies, computes the counts expected if the two variables were independent, and measures the gap with the χ² statistic and its p-value. Then it asks what a "dependent" verdict does and does not say about cause and effect.

What this page is about. Two variables are independent when knowing the value of one tells you nothing about the other. In probability this is an exact property of a joint distribution. In statistics we only see a finite sample, where the property never holds exactly even if it holds in the population: the question becomes whether the departure we see is larger than sampling noise alone would produce. The contingency table organises the evidence, the expected counts describe the "nothing going on" world, and χ² measures how far the data are from it.

1. Independence in probability and in statistics

1.1 In probability

Definition (events). Two events A and B in a probability space are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). When P(B) > 0 this is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A): learning that B happened does not change the probability of A.
Definition (random variables). Two discrete random variables X and F are independent if every pair of events they define is independent, i.e. their joint probability mass function factorises into the product of the marginals:
p(x, f) = P(X = x, F = f) = pX(x) · pF(f) for all x and f
For continuous variables the same holds with densities, or in general with the CDFs: FX,F(x, f) = FX(x)·FF(f).

Equivalent forms, each useful in a different place below:

  • Conditional = marginal: P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f) for every x with P(X = x) > 0. All the conditional distributions of F given X are the same distribution, and it equals the marginal of F. The property is symmetric, so the same holds with the roles of X and F swapped.
  • Expectations factorise: E[g(X)·h(F)] = E[g(X)]·E[h(F)] for all bounded functions g, h. In particular independent numeric variables are uncorrelated. The converse fails: zero correlation only rules out a linear relation (X uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and F = X² have Cov = 0 but F is a function of X).
  • Dependence is simply the negation: there is at least one cell (x, f) where p(x, f) ≠ pX(x)·pF(f).

1.2 In statistics

Definition (contingency table). For n observations of two categorical variables, X with r categories x1…xr and F with c categories f1…fc, the contingency table is the r × c table of absolute joint frequencies nij = #{k : xk = xi, fk = fj}. Its row totals ni· and column totals n·j are the absolute marginal frequencies, and Σ nij = n.
relative joint fij = nij / n marginal fi· = ni· / n f·j = n·j / n conditional of F|X fj|i = nij / ni· (row profiles: each row sums to 1) conditional of X|F fi|j = nij / n·j (column profiles: each column sums to 1)
Definition (statistical independence). X and F are statistically independent in the data (perfect independence, in the descriptive sense) if the relative joint frequencies are the products of the marginal ones: fij = fi··f·j for all i, j. Equivalently all row profiles coincide (fj|i = f·j), or all column profiles coincide, or
nij = ni· · n·j / n =: ñij (the expected or theoretical frequency)

This is the probabilistic definition with probabilities replaced by relative frequencies. The two are tied by the law of large numbers: if the n records are i.i.d. draws from p(x, f), then fij → p(xi, fj), fi· → pX(xi) and f·j → pF(fj). So under probabilistic independence fij − fi·f·j → 0, but for finite n it is almost never exactly 0 (with integer counts, ñij is usually not even an integer). Descriptive independence is therefore a reference point rather than something we expect to observe, and we need a measure of how far the table is from it, and a scale telling us how far is too far to blame on chance.

1.3 The measure: Pearson's χ²

Definition. The chi-square statistic sums the squared gaps between observed and expected counts, each scaled by the expected count:
χ² = Σi Σj (nij − ñij)² / ñij χ² = 0 ⇔ descriptive independence

Why divide by ñij: a cell count is roughly Poisson, so its variance is about its mean, and (nij − ñij)/√ñij (the Pearson residual) is a gap measured in standard deviations. χ² is the sum of their squares.

Its null distribution. Under the hypothesis H0: "X and F are independent", and for large n, χ² is approximately distributed as a chi-square with

df = (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom

There are r·c cells, but the expected counts were built to have the same margins as the data: r row totals and c column totals, of which r + c − 1 are independent (they share the grand total n). That leaves rc − (r + c − 1) = (r − 1)(c − 1) residuals free to vary. The p-value is P(χ²df ≥ observed χ²): the probability, if H0 were true, of a table at least this far from independence. The approximation is reliable when the expected counts are not too small; the usual rule is ñij ≥ 5 in (nearly) all cells. Section 5.2 checks it without any approximation, by permutation.

Definition (strength). χ² grows with n for a fixed pattern of dependence, so it measures evidence, not strength. Cramér's V = √(χ² / (n·(min(r, c) − 1))) removes n and the table size: V = 0 under descriptive independence and V = 1 for a perfect association (in a square table: each category of X goes with a single category of F).

4. Expected frequencies under independence

ñij = ni··n·j/n is the table with the same margins as the data and perfectly proportional rows: the world where X carries no information about F. Each cell shows the observed count, the expected count (grey) and its contribution (nij − ñij)²/ñij to χ². The background colour is the adjusted residual (nij − ñij)/√(ñij(1 − fi·)(1 − f·j)), approximately N(0, 1) under H0: blue = more than expected, orange = fewer; |value| > 2 is unusual, |value| > 3 very unusual.

5. The χ² statistic and the p-value

The p-value is computed from the regularized upper incomplete gamma function Q(df/2, χ²/2), with a series for small arguments and a continued fraction for large ones; the critical value χ²1−α, df is found by bisection on the CDF. Both agree with scipy.stats to about 14 significant digits on the datasets here. The likelihood-ratio statistic G = 2 Σ nij ln(nij/ñij) has the same asymptotic distribution and is shown for comparison.

5.1 The computation in code

JavaScript (the function used on this page, simplified)

const E = rowTot.map(a => colTot.map(b => a * b / n));
let chi2 = 0;
for (let i = 0; i < r; i++)
  for (let j = 0; j < c; j++)
    chi2 += (O[i][j] - E[i][j]) ** 2 / E[i][j];
const df = (r - 1) * (c - 1);
const p  = gammaQ(df / 2, chi2 / 2);   // P(χ²_df ≥ chi2)
const V  = Math.sqrt(chi2 / (n * (Math.min(r, c) - 1)));

Python, from raw records, checked against SciPy

from collections import Counter
from scipy.stats import chi2 as chi2_dist, chi2_contingency

def independence(records):
    xs = sorted({x for x, _ in records}); fs = sorted({f for _, f in records})
    cnt = Counter(records)
    O = [[cnt[(x, f)] for f in fs] for x in xs]
    n = len(records)
    rt = [sum(r) for r in O]; ct = [sum(c) for c in zip(*O)]
    E = [[a * b / n for b in ct] for a in rt]
    chi2 = sum((o - e) ** 2 / e for Or, Er in zip(O, E) for o, e in zip(Or, Er))
    df = (len(xs) - 1) * (len(fs) - 1)
    return O, E, chi2, df, chi2_dist.sf(chi2, df)

# same numbers as chi2_contingency(O, correction=False)

5.2 A check without the χ² approximation: the permutation test

If X and F are independent, the pairing between the x column and the f column of the records is arbitrary. Shuffling the f column at random destroys any link while keeping both margins exactly as observed; each shuffle gives a table drawn from H0. Repeating it B times gives the exact null distribution of χ² for these margins, up to Monte Carlo error, with no large-sample assumption. The permutation p-value is (#{shuffled χ² ≥ observed} + 1)/(B + 1).

5.3 Does the test keep its promise?

A test at level α should reject a true H0 about α of the time (type I error), and reject a false one as often as possible (power). With the synthetic log both cases can be generated: 1 000 independent logs of the chosen size at λ = 0 (independence holds) and at the chosen λ.

7. Statistical association is not causation

A significant χ² establishes, with a controlled error rate, that X and F are associated: in the population, the distribution of F differs across values of X. It says nothing about why. The test is symmetric in X and F, so it cannot even tell the direction; and it is computed on a two-way table, so it cannot see any third variable. The usual ways an association arises without X causing F:

  • Confounding. A third variable Z influences both X and F. Conditioning on Z can weaken, remove or even reverse the association (Simpson's paradox, below).
  • Reverse causation. F causes X, not the other way round; the table looks the same.
  • Selection. The way records entered the dataset depends on both variables (only alerts that were reviewed get logged, only survivors are interviewed), creating an association that does not exist in the population.
  • Chance. At α = 0.05, one test in twenty on independent variables comes out "significant"; running many tests and reporting the significant ones guarantees false findings.

Conversely, independence in a table does not prove the absence of a causal effect: two effects of opposite sign through different paths can cancel in the margins. Establishing causation needs a design (a randomised experiment, where X is assigned by a coin and therefore independent of every Z) or an explicit causal model that says which variables to condition on, plus subject-matter arguments (temporal order, mechanism, dose–response, consistency across studies).

7.1 Simpson's paradox: Berkeley admissions, 1973

Aggregated over departments, men were admitted at a visibly higher rate than women, and the χ² test is overwhelming. Split by department, the picture changes:

The confounder is the department. Women applied mostly to departments with low admission rates for everyone (C–F), men mostly to departments with high rates (A, B). Department is associated with sex (who applies where) and with admission (how selective it is), and this alone produces the aggregate association. The aggregate test is not wrong: sex and admission are associated among these applicants. What is wrong is reading it as "the admission process discriminates by sex", which is a causal statement the two-way table cannot support.

7.2 Reading the other datasets

  • Titanic. Class and survival are strongly associated. Plausible mechanisms are real (cabin location relative to the lifeboats, order of evacuation), but class is also associated with sex and age (the crew was almost entirely adult men, and "women and children first" was applied), so part of the class effect is a sex/age effect. The two-way table cannot separate them; a three-way analysis or a model with sex and age can, and only partially.
  • Hair × eye colour. A clear association with no causal arrow between the two at all: both depend on how much melanin the body produces, which is governed by partly shared pigmentation genes. It is association through a common cause, and both variables are also tied to ancestry.
  • Synthetic IDS log. Here we wrote the generating process, so we know the association is built in. Even so, "SSH causes malicious verdicts" is the wrong reading: attackers choose to target SSH and FTP (selection of targets by a third agent), and the IDS itself may flag those protocols more readily (a property of the measuring instrument, not of the traffic). Same table, three causal stories.
